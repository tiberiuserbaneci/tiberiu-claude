## CAPTION

Three deals sat at "maybe" for a month. Claude found the objection holding up the one that
closed, and nobody had ever said it to me out loud.

That is the part I had wrong about stalled deals. I assumed silence meant priority, budget or a
competitor, so I did what everyone does: waited a polite interval, sent a follow up asking if
they had any thoughts, and waited again. Seven days of reading instead of chasing turned one of
the three, and it was not the one I would have bet on.

- - -

Four things came out of it:

→ The deal I thought was alive was not, and the one I had half written off was. I was ranking
on how warm the last call felt. The notes said something different, and the notes were right.

→ The objection was in the transcript, not the reply. Nobody says "your onboarding worries me".
They say "let me take this to the team", and the real thing sits two calls back in something
they already told me.

→ The follow up asking for thoughts is not a follow up. It is a request for free labour: it
asks the buyer to do the work of restarting the deal, and it is the message I have sent
hundreds of times.

→ The proposal was priced against the deals that actually closed before it, not against what I
felt like charging at midnight. That is the number I have always been worst at, and it is the
one with the least excuse for being a guess.

- - -

Here is what I did not expect: the work was all reading, and none of it was new information.

Every fact that turned that deal was already in my own notes. It had been there for a month.
What I did not have was the seven uninterrupted hours to go back through three deals worth of
calls, threads and half-written proposals and find the one sentence that mattered. So the deal
did not stall because I lacked a tactic. It stalled because reading is invisible work and it
never gets scheduled.

I still send everything. The close plan arrived with a date on it, I changed two lines, and it
went out in my own words. Nothing sends itself and nothing should.
