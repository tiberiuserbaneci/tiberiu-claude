## CAPTION

A fourteen page agreement arrived on a Friday afternoon and Claude found three clauses in it
that were not standard. I had already decided to sign it.

That decision is the honest part. I was not being reckless, I was doing what every founder I
know does: the deal was agreed, the document was long, the alternative was losing a weekend to
it, and the summary email said it was our standard terms. The summary email described a
different document than the one attached to it.

- - -

What the three clauses were, and why none of them looked like a problem:

→ Auto-renewal with a ninety day notice window. Nothing unusual on its own. It matters because
the notice window opened before I would have reviewed anything, so the first renewal was
effectively automatic and I would have called that my own fault.

→ A change of control clause that let them terminate on our side of an acquisition. Standard
language pointed the wrong direction, and it is the clause that only ever matters on the one
day you cannot afford to be renegotiating.

→ Unlimited liability carve-outs sitting under a capped liability heading. The cap was real.
The list of things it did not cover was longer than the list it did.

- - -

None of that needed a lawyer to spot. It needed forty uninterrupted minutes and someone who had
actually read the whole thing, and on a Friday afternoon before a call I was late for, I was
never going to be that person. That is the real failure mode with contracts at our size. It is
not that founders cannot read a clause. It is that the document always arrives at the exact
moment reading it costs the most, and the cost of skimming it is invisible for about eighteen
months.

Two of the three came back agreed without an argument, which tells you they were not there
because anyone fought for them. They were there because nobody had pushed back on them before.

What went back was a redline in my own words, so it read like someone who had read it rather
than a template. I read it once and sent it unsigned. That is the whole workflow: it reads, it
flags, it drafts, and I decide. It does not sign anything, it is not legal advice, and it
certifies nothing. Anyone selling you the version where the software makes you safe is selling
you the risk back with extra steps.
