## CAPTION

We hired one person last month and three software bills went up before they had a login.
That is what per-seat pricing does: it turns every new hire into a procurement decision.
And the founder at a team of four is the person who has to explain why the tool everyone
already uses suddenly costs more.

The software did not work harder. The server load did not double. The only thing that
changed was the headcount in a field the invoice calls seats, a word that makes it sound
like you are buying furniture rather than access.

- - -

What the twelve hours looked like, and why the real cost was never the dollars:

→ Hour one: the invoice arrived with a seat count that had incremented overnight. Nothing
else changed. It was just there, three to four and a total that went with it. Ten minutes
on a billing portal instead of anything that moved the company forward.

→ Hour three: the spreadsheet started, because someone had to decide who actually needs a
license. The founder does not have an operations person to hand this to, so the founder
does it, and every seat feels like a vote on whether a team member is essential. That is
the cost nobody puts on the invoice. The cost is the time spent deciding who deserves the
seat, which is a conversation that does not make anyone more productive.

→ Hour six: a team lead asked about trying a new tool, and before I heard the use case I
multiplied the price by the headcount. I said let me check the budget, which kills momentum
without deciding anything. The tool might have been useful. I will never know, because the
conversation was about seats before it was about value.

→ Hour twelve: the flat-price alternative was already on the market when I signed the
per-seat contract. I chose it because the monthly number looked smaller and I did not model
what happens at six people, eight people, twelve people. That is the mistake per-seat
pricing relies on. It does not deceive you. It asks you to ignore the curve, and at the
stage where every hire changes the company, ignoring the curve is the only thing easier than
reading it.

- - -

That is the whole argument: per-seat pricing is not a scam and it is not evil. It is a
model that makes sense at scale and stops making sense at the exact moment you stop having
a procurement function. For teams under ten, the flat-price alternative removes one variable
from every hiring conversation. It does not replace your judgement. It removes the seat
count from it.
