Losing one is not the interesting part. Everybody loses one. The interesting part is that I
would have told you it ended in month ten, over price, and every single piece of that sentence

- - -

→ When did it actually end. Month 3, on a ticket that took 9 days to answer. Every message
after it is shorter than the one before, and the cancellation in month 11 was admin.

→ What did I read as a compliment. Twice they said no rush, which meant it had stopped
mattering. Once they asked about a cheaper plan. Nobody new from their side ever joined the
thread.

→ Who stopped replying first. The person who signed left in month 5. Everything after that was
a user rather than a buyer, and I never once asked who had replaced them.

→ Was it price. They said budget, once, at the end. Usage had been flat for 4 months before
that and they renewed two other tools in the same quarter.

→ Which current customers look like this one did in month 3. Two, on the same usage curve, both
quiet for three weeks. That is the only part of this that is worth anything.

- - -

The number I watch now is days to first answer, and it is the only metric I added all year. Not
because slow support loses customers, which everybody knows, but because the first slow ticket
is the moment the relationship changes register and neither side says so. They stop expecting
much and you stop noticing that they stopped. Eight months of politeness later, somebody sends
a one line email about budget.
What I would say to anybody about to do this with their own lost customer: read the thread
in one sitting rather than remembering it. Memory edits. It keeps the ending and throws away
the eight months of small signals that made the ending inevitable, and it always parks the
cause somewhere convenient, which in my case was price. Price is where founders put a loss
they have not understood yet, because price is the one reason that does not require you to
have done anything wrong.
