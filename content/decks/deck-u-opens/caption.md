## LINKEDIN CAPTION  (carousel 1080x1350)

The most expensive tool in my stack was the one I opened least, and the cheapest was the one I
opened 22 times a week. Claude replaced the one in the middle that only existed to hand me a
number.

- - -

Nobody cancels software because of the price. They cancel it because somebody finally counted.
So I counted, for a month: every time I opened every tool I pay for, with no judgement attached
and no intention of cancelling anything. The chart came out almost perfectly inverted, which I
did not expect and which is the only reason this is worth posting rather than another list of
tools somebody recommends.

- - -

→ 22 opens a week for the thing I actually work in, every working day, several times a day. It
has the smallest invoice of the seven.

→ 6 opens a week for scheduling. Not the meetings. Finding the time for the meetings, which is
a job I had not noticed I bought a tool for.

→ 4 opens for the CRM, and 3 of those were me updating it rather than reading it. A system you
maintain and never consult is a chore with a login screen.

→ 2 opens for the analytics dashboard, both on a Monday, both to look at a number I could have
been handed. That one is gone now and the number arrives instead.

→ 1 open for the most expensive seat on the list, kept because cancelling felt like admitting
something. That is not a tool cost, it is an identity cost, and I paid it for a year.

- - -

The seventh had 0 opens in the month I was counting, and I found it on the card statement rather
than in my head, which is the entire reason the exercise exists. Three questions I ask before
renewing anything now: how many times did I open it last month, what would happen on the day it
disappeared, and is it doing a job or storing a job. The last one catches most of them. A tool
that only holds information you put into it by hand is not doing work, it is charging rent on
your own data.
What I would not do is turn this into a purge. Two of the seven survived the audit easily and
I pay more for them now than I did then, because counting told me they are where the work
happens. The exercise is not about spending less, it is about knowing which line on the card
is doing something, and the reason almost nobody knows is that it takes a month of noticing
rather than an afternoon of deciding.
