## LINKEDIN CAPTION  (carousel 1080x1350)

Four seconds of silence is the cheapest question in sales and almost nobody can hold it. Claude
prepares the other nine minutes so I have the nerve to spend those four doing nothing.

- - -

A first call is decided before anyone says what their product does, which is why the founders
who lose them are usually the ones who prepared the demo and not the questions. I ran mine
badly for about two years. Talking, mostly, because talking feels like effort and silence feels
like failing. What changed is not charisma and it is not a script. It is seven things that
happen in the first ten minutes, and every one of them is something I ask rather than something
I say.

- - -

→ Why did they take this call. Something happened recently that made a stranger's email worth
20 minutes, and whatever that is, that is the deal. If nobody can name it, there is no urgency.

→ Who else has to agree. Asked in minute 3, not week 3. If the answer is vague, this is a
conversation rather than a deal, and I stop selling and start mapping.

→ What are they doing about it today. Every problem already has a solution, usually a person
and a spreadsheet. That is the competitor, not the other software.

→ Get them to say the number. Hours, deals, money. If they cannot put a number on it, it is an
irritation rather than a problem, and irritations do not get budget.

→ Say what makes it wrong for them, out loud, early. It costs one bad fit deal and it buys
every good one, because naming a real limitation is what makes the rest believable.

- - -

The last two are the ones that changed the numbers. Four seconds of silence after they answer,
because the second thing they say is the true one and the first is the version they prepared on
the way in. And booking the next step on the call itself, with a date, a person, and what has
to be true by then. Everything I have ever left to email has died in email, and it died
politely, which is worse.
The reason all seven survive is that none of them is a technique. They are just the
questions a person would ask if they were genuinely trying to work out whether this is
a fit, rather than trying to get to the demo. That distinction is audible. Buyers have
sat through hundreds of calls where somebody was waiting for their turn to speak, and
they can hear the difference inside two minutes, which is roughly when they decide
whether the next twenty are worth giving you.
